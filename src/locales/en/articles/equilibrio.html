This calculator tells you how many units you have to sell to cover all your costs, and what sales that means. Enter the fixed costs for the period, the selling price per unit and the variable cost per unit, and you get the contribution margin of each unit, the number of units needed to reach break-even and the corresponding sales. Optionally, you can type how many units you expect to sell and see the projected result.

How to use the calculator

Enter four figures, three required and one optional:

  • Fixed costs for the period. The costs you pay even if you sell little or nothing: rent, insurance, subscriptions, fixed salaries. They must cover the same period as the sales you want to analyse, for example one month.
  • Selling price per unit. What you charge for each unit, before tax.
  • Variable cost per unit. What it costs to produce or sell each unit: raw materials, shipping, sales commission.
  • Units you expect to sell (optional). If you fill it in, you will see the profit or loss at that volume. If you leave it empty, it is not calculated.

What is the break-even point

The break-even point is the sales volume at which revenue equals total costs: there is neither profit nor loss. Below that volume the business loses money; above it, each additional unit adds profit. It is calculated with the contribution margin, which is what is left of each sale after paying the variable costs of that unit.

Its limit is that it assumes a single product with a constant price and variable cost. In a business with several products, or with prices that change with volume, it is an approximation.

Break-even formulas

The contribution margin per unit is the difference between the price and the variable cost:

Contribution margin = Selling price − Variable cost

The units needed come from spreading the fixed costs over that margin:

Units = Fixed costs ÷ Contribution margin

Since units cannot be split, the result is always rounded up. The corresponding sales are calculated with the rounded units:

Sales = Rounded units × Selling price

And the result at any volume is:

Result = Units × Contribution margin − Fixed costs

Control example: fixed costs of 2,000, a price of 50 and a variable cost of 30. The contribution margin is 20 and the units are 2,000 ÷ 20 = 100. Sales at break-even are 100 × 50 = 5,000. With 150 units, the result would be 150 × 20 − 2,000 = 1,000 of profit.

When the result is not a whole number

With fixed costs of 3,500, a price of 12.50 and a variable cost of 4.50, the contribution margin is 8 and the calculation gives 437.5 units. With 437 units there is still a loss: 437 × 8 = 3,496, which does not cover 3,500. You have to sell 438 and the sales are 438 × 12.50 = 5,475.

Item Calculation Result
Contribution margin 12.50 − 4.50 8
Units before rounding 3,500 ÷ 8 437.5
Units needed rounded up 438
Sales 438 × 12.50 5,475

Common mistakes when calculating break-even

Mixing periods

If fixed costs are annual and the price and units are monthly, the result makes no sense. Use fixed costs for the same period as the sales you want to analyse.

Putting variable costs among the fixed ones, or the other way round

A sales commission or the cost of raw materials is variable. Rent or an insurance policy is fixed. If you put a cost in the wrong group, the break-even point changes. If a cost is mixed, split its fixed part and its variable part.

Using the margin on sales instead of the contribution margin as an amount

The contribution margin per unit is measured as an amount. If you divide fixed costs by a percentage, you get sales, not units. The calculator also shows the contribution ratio, which is the percentage of the price left after the variable cost, in case you prefer to work out sales directly.

Forgetting that the price must exceed the variable cost

If the price is equal to or below the variable cost, each unit sold earns nothing or loses money. No volume covers the fixed costs, and the calculator warns you instead of giving an impossible figure.

Rounding down

With 437.5 units, selling 437 is not enough. Indivisible units are always rounded up.

A complete example, step by step

A bakery has 1,800 a month in fixed costs. Each cake sells for 24 and costs 9 in ingredients and packaging.

  1. Contribution margin: 24 − 9 = 15 per cake.
  2. Units: 1,800 ÷ 15 = 120 cakes a month.
  3. Sales: 120 × 24 = 2,880 a month.
  4. With 100 cakes: 100 × 15 − 1,800 = −300, a loss.
  5. With 150 cakes: 150 × 15 − 1,800 = 450 of profit.

If the price rises to 27, the contribution margin becomes 18 and the break-even point falls to 100 cakes. With the profit margin calculator you can see how the margin changes when you change the price.

How to read the result

  • Compare it with your real capacity. If break-even requires more units than you can produce or sell, you need to change the price, cut costs or rethink the business.
  • Try scenarios. Small changes in the price or the variable cost move the units needed a lot, because the contribution margin is the divisor.
  • Remember what it leaves out. Break-even covers costs; it is not a profit target. To see how much profit a given revenue leaves, use the gross and net margin calculator.
  • Review costs often. A new rent or a change of supplier changes the result.
