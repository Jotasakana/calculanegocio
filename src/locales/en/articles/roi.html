This calculator measures how much you have gained or lost on an investment compared with what you put in. Enter the initial investment and the total return, including the investment recovered, and you get the net profit or loss, the ROI as a percentage and a simple reading of the result.

How to use the calculator

Enter two figures:

  • Initial investment. All the money you put in: the main amount and the related costs (fees, installation, training, advertising).
  • Total return. Everything you have recovered, including the investment. If you invested 1,000 and now have 1,300, the total return is 1,300, not 300.

Results are rounded to two decimals when displayed, but the internal calculations are not rounded. If the initial investment is zero, the ROI cannot be calculated and the calculator tells you so.

What is ROI

ROI stands for return on investment. It expresses the net profit of an investment as a percentage of what was invested. An ROI of 30% means that for every 100 invested you made 30 of profit, in addition to getting the 100 back.

It is a useful indicator for comparing investments of different sizes, but it has important limits: it does not include time, it does not distinguish when the income arrives and it depends on which costs were counted.

ROI formulas

The net profit or loss is the difference between what you recovered and what you invested:

Net profit = Total return − Initial investment

ROI divides that result by the investment:

ROI (%) = (Total return − Initial investment) ÷ Initial investment × 100

Control example: you invest 1,000 and recover 1,300. The net profit is 300 and the ROI is 300 ÷ 1,000 × 100 = 30%.

How to read it

Investment Total return Net profit ROI
1,000 1,300 300 30%
800 2,000 1,200 150%
2,500 2,500 0 0%
5,000 4,000 −1,000 −20%
1,000 0 −1,000 −100%

A positive ROI means a gain, zero means you only got back what you invested and a negative ROI means a loss. The lowest possible value is −100%, which means losing everything invested.

ROI, ROAS, IRR and annualised return

These are four different indicators that are often confused:

  • ROAS divides the revenue a campaign generates by its advertising spend. It uses revenue, not profit. With 500 of advertising that generates 2,000 in sales, the ROAS is 4. If those sales cost 1,200 in goods, the profit is 300 and the ROI on that 500 is 60%.
  • IRR is the annual rate of return that equates the cash flows of an investment with payments and receipts at different times. This calculator does not produce it.
  • Annualised return converts an ROI to a one-year basis. A 30% return over three years is equivalent to 9.14% a year compounded, because 1.30 to the power of 1/3 is 1.0914. This result is outside this calculator.
  • Margin compares profit with the sales of a product, not with an investment.

Common mistakes when calculating ROI

Entering only the profit as the total return

If you enter 300 instead of 1,300, the ROI would come out at −70%. The total return always includes what was invested. The calculator subtracts the investment for you.

Forgetting related costs

Fees, maintenance, training or advertising are part of the investment. If you leave them out, the ROI comes out higher than the real one.

Comparing the ROI of different time frames

30% in one month and 30% in five years give the same ROI, but they are not comparable. Before choosing between two investments, compare the time each one took too.

Confusing ROI with ROAS

ROAS measures revenue per unit of advertising; it does not deduct the cost of what was sold. A campaign with a high ROAS can have a low or negative ROI.

Ignoring risk

A past ROI does not guarantee the future, and two investments with the same ROI can carry very different risks. ROI describes an outcome, not its probability.

A complete example, step by step

A business buys a machine for 4,000, pays 500 for installation and training and, over two years, earns 6,300 in net income thanks to it.

  1. Initial investment: 4,000 + 500 = 4,500.
  2. Total return: 6,300.
  3. Net profit: 6,300 − 4,500 = 1,800.
  4. ROI: 1,800 ÷ 4,500 × 100 = 40%.
  5. Return per unit invested: 6,300 ÷ 4,500 = 1.40.

That 40% was earned over two years: it is an ROI, not an annual return. To find out how many units you must sell to cover fixed costs, use the break-even calculator.

How to read the result

  • Compare it with alternatives. An ROI of 5% can be good or bad depending on the time frame and the risk of other options.
  • Check which costs you counted. A reliable ROI starts from a complete investment.
  • Add the time factor. Ask yourself how long it took to get the result.
  • Do not use it as your only measure. Complement it with margin, break-even and the liquidity of your business.
